Offering salt looks like the simplest job on the farm: hang a block, fill a feeder, and let the animals take what they need. When salt goes wrong, the salt is usually not the cause. A frozen waterer, a crowded pen, or a group of animals that had no salt for a week and then found an open bag all matter more than the product itself.

This guide covers the handling decisions that keep salt a routine part of feeding: water supply, realistic intake, gradual introduction, and a feeder that does not turn a rock salt block into a puddle.

Water comes first

The Merck Veterinary Manual's entry on salt toxicosis is direct about what matters most: when an animal's sodium regulation is intact and fresh drinking water is available continuously, salt toxicosis is unlikely. Risk climbs with the conditions that interrupt water — a failed waterer, overcrowding, frozen or unpalatable water, or a sudden restriction of the supply.

That ordering should shape how you manage salt. Before a block, pouch or bag goes into a pen, walk the water source. If you cannot keep clean water available through a freeze, a power outage or a weekend away, fix that before adding more salt.

The same source notes that horses are rarely affected by classic salt toxicosis, but can be after salt intake rises and water is then restricted. It is the combination that creates the problem, not salt on its own.

How much water a salt-fed animal may need

  • Water demand around salt: Oklahoma State Extension estimates that water use can rise 50–75 %, or about 5 additional gallons of water for every pound of salt consumed, when salt is used to limit intake in a self-fed supplement. The same guidance advises caution when the water supply runs above 5,000 ppm total dissolved solids.
  • Read that figure in context: it belongs to salt-limited feeding, where salt is deliberately used to hold intake down. It is not a water allowance for routine free-choice salt.
  • Horses: the Merck Veterinary Manual recommends unlimited access to clean water. Needs rise with dry feed, work, heat, sweating and lactation, and sweat loss can increase water needs by 50–200 %. For a horse that has sweated heavily or is dehydrated, the manual names a properly balanced electrolyte supplement, and says that if electrolytes are added to drinking water, plain water should be offered at the same time. It also discourages prolonged supplementation when it is not needed.

Sudden access after a salt gap

The most common handling mistake is not a measured dose. It is a jump. Penn State Extension describes a herd of 30 dairy cows that consumed a 50 lb bag of loose salt in a single afternoon after fresh grazing access was introduced. Three cows could not rise, others had loose manure, and several animals died. Water was available in that case, and the amount any one cow ate was never measured.

Treat that as a case report rather than a threshold. It does not establish how much salt is dangerous, and it does not describe a normal feeding level. What it shows is a pattern worth avoiding: animals that have been without salt, given large sudden access to it. Cautious reintroduction and regular monitoring of intake are the practical carryover from that report.

Knowing what ordinary intake looks like makes an unusual one easier to spot. Published estimates differ by source and by feeding setup, so read them as estimates rather than fixed doses. Oklahoma State Extension puts mature cattle at less than 1 oz of salt per head per day; University of Georgia Extension gives a rule of thumb of 0.005–0.010 % of body weight, about 1.0–1.9 oz for a 1,200 lb cow; and Missouri Extension observed free-choice loose salt intake of ¾–1⅓ oz per head per day, with roughly half that taken from a block in the comparison it cites.

Horse intake varies more widely still. The Merck Veterinary Manual cites a National Research Council ration recommendation of 1.6–1.8 g of salt per kg of feed dry matter, and reports 19–134 g per day in one study. Purdue Extension gives older general guidance of about 1 oz per day, and Oklahoma State Extension says 2 tablespoons is generally sufficient for an exercising horse, with more likely needed in hot, humid conditions.

If a bag or block disappears faster than those ranges suggest, treat it as a management question and inspect the pen the same day: water first, then the feeder, then how many animals are sharing it.

What the toxicology numbers do and do not mean

The Merck Veterinary Manual reports an acute oral lethal dose of about 2.2 g of salt per kg of body weight for cattle and horses. That is a veterinary toxicology figure, not a feeding limit and not a level anyone should approach on purpose. It exists so a clinician can reason about an exposure after the fact.

The same entry lists a serum or cerebrospinal fluid sodium above 160 mEq/L as suggesting toxicosis. That is a diagnostic reading, not a target to feed toward. It also warns that restoring water and electrolytes too quickly can worsen brain swelling. A suspected poisoning case is a call to a veterinarian; it is not a situation for a home remedy, and pushing water on an affected animal at speed can do harm.

Handling salt at the feeder

The habits below come from extension guidance on keeping free-choice salt dry, plus the shelf logic that salt dissolves in water and is easier to use when it has not had to sit through the weather.

  • Shelter the feeder. University of Georgia Extension says a mineral feeder should keep the product dry, and Nebraska Extension covers keeping free-choice feeders out of rain and wind. Loose salt that cakes in a wet feeder is harder for animals to take.
  • Check it at least weekly. University of Georgia Extension recommends a weekly check. Look at how fast the salt is going, whether rain has reached it, and whether every animal can reach it.
  • Get blocks off the ground and out of standing water, sheltered from direct rain. Salt dissolves in water, so a block sitting in a puddle is a block leaving the farm.
  • Introduce salt gradually to animals that have been without it, and watch intake for the first days after it goes out.
  • Placement is a management decision. University of Georgia Extension lists areas near water, shaded loafing areas and good grazing areas among the options, while South Dakota State University Extension advises placing blocks away from natural water and riparian areas in some pasture setups so grazing pressure spreads out. What is not established is a universal rule that a block must sit beside water, or a service life after which a block must be replaced. Judge placement by your own pasture and water layout.

A short checklist before you refill

Walk the water first and confirm it is clean, unfrozen and reachable by every animal in the group. Then check the feeder: dry inside, checked on a schedule, off the ground, and a manageable distance from where the animals already gather. Then look at the salt itself and compare how quickly it is going against the intake ranges above.
