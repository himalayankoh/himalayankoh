Salt has an unusual storage story. It does not go bad the way flour or coffee does, and it does not need a date on the lid. What it does do is react to moisture, and that one fact explains almost every storage problem people run into, at home and outdoors.

This guide covers both halves of the same question. The first half is the salt sitting on your kitchen shelf. The second half is the salt you put out for horses or cattle, which lives in the weather instead of in a cupboard.

Moisture is the storage problem, not the calendar

Sodium chloride is moisture-sensitive. It takes up water vapor from the air, and when that moisture is later released the salt cakes into lumps. A cargo-handling reference on salt puts the figures at appreciable moisture uptake beginning around 74% relative humidity at 20°C, with the salt dissolving at about 75%. Those are transport-industry reference conditions rather than a kitchen threshold, but they describe the pattern you see at home: the salt stays free-flowing in dry air and starts to clump as the air around it gets humid.

Utah State University Extension advises storing opened salt away from moisture because moisture is what causes caking, and it describes iodized salt as having an indefinite shelf life once it is protected from moisture and contamination. Temperature matters less than people expect. A sealed container in a dry cupboard keeps salt usable for years; an open bag beside a simmering pot works against you, because cooking steam is moisture in its most convenient form.

One useful conclusion follows from all of this: caking is a sign that the salt is holding water, not a sign that it has spoiled. A lump of salt in a jar is still salt.

Storing edible salt at home

The habits that work are few enough to count. Keep the salt in a clean container that closes tightly, keep that container in a dry place, take salt out with a dry spoon, and close the container again after every use.

Anti-caking agents are the other half of the picture. Salt is sold both with and without them. The United States permits specified anti-caking agents for food use (21 CFR Part 172, Subpart E), and the salt labeling rule allows a "free flowing" statement on salt that contains them (21 CFR 100.155). Salt without an anti-caking agent can clump when it takes up moisture, and that clumping on its own is not contamination or a quality defect. It is what a salt with nothing added does in humid air.

Two small practices make a container last longer. Transfer the salt once, if you transfer it at all, and keep the original label or a photo of it with the new container, so you still have the product name, the package size and any handling directions when it is time to reorder. Follow the directions printed on your own package; general storage advice does not replace them.

When the salt lives outdoors

Livestock salt faces a harder environment than kitchen salt, and the same logic holds: dry product is usable product. University of Georgia Extension guidance for mineral feeders says a feeder should keep the mineral dry, and recommends checking feeders at least weekly. Loose salt that cakes in a wet feeder is harder for animals to take, which is the practical cost of a feeder that collects rain. Nebraska Extension makes the same point from the other direction, focusing on protecting the feeder from rain and wind rather than on the product inside it.

For a block or a lick, the placement advice is easier to state than the durability questions are to answer. A block kept off the ground, clear of standing water and sheltered from direct rain lasts longer, simply because salt dissolves in water. Take that as practical guidance: no extension source sets a universal rule for mounting a block, and none gives a rain-related replacement interval.

Where to put it is a management decision, and the extension sources do not agree on a single answer. University of Georgia Extension lists areas near water, shaded loafing areas and good grazing areas as options, while South Dakota State University Extension advises placing blocks away from natural water and riparian areas in some pasture setups so livestock spread their grazing pressure instead of concentrating it along a stream bank. Choose the placement that matches what you are managing for, and keep the feeder dry either way.

What the sources do not offer is a service life. There is no verified replacement interval for an outdoor salt block, because durability depends on rainfall, humidity, how the block is mounted and how heavily the animals use it. Inspect the block, replace it when it has dissolved or broken apart, and keep the mounting sound.

If you use a block for cooking or serving

Blocks used in the kitchen follow maker-level guidance rather than food-safety regulation. The published instructions from salt-block sellers and cooks agree on the main points: make sure the block is completely dry before you heat it, raise the heat gradually instead of putting a cold block straight onto high heat, and expect fissures to develop with use. A slab is a consumable piece of rock, not a pan you keep forever. The Meadow salt-block guide and The Splendid Table's salt-block cooking explainer both take that approach.

Cleaning follows the same logic. Let the slab cool completely first, then clean it with minimal water, no soap and no dishwasher. Follow the directions that come with your block where they are more specific than this.

Keep the boundary in mind. A block is a cooking surface that seasons food, and the guidance above is culinary instruction from the people who sell and cook on slabs. It is not a validated method for preserving food, and salt on its own does not make food safe. For anything involving curing or long storage, use a tested recipe and follow it exactly.

The short version

Keep edible salt dry, sealed and clean, and read caking as a moisture signal rather than an expiration date. Keep livestock salt out of standing water and rain, check the feeder weekly, and inspect the block instead of waiting for a replacement schedule that does not exist.
