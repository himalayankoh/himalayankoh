Salt arrives with paperwork more often than most pantry ingredients. A supplier sends a certificate of analysis, a specification sheet, or a line of numbers in an email, and the reader must work out whether the salt suits the job in front of them. Chefs check a cooking salt this way; livestock owners compare a feed salt against the specification their nutrition plan calls for.

The numbers are rarely the hard part. A salt report can be written against different standards, measured on different bases, and published in different units, so the same figure can look reassuring or alarming depending on the page you are holding.

Two documents, two different jobs

The first thing to establish is which document you are reading. A certificate of analysis reports what a specific lot of salt actually tested at. A specification sheet states the limits or targets the product is expected to meet. Neither has one universal format, so the practical habit is to read the fields rather than the cover.

The distinction matters because the documents answer different questions. A certificate describes a sample from one lot and no other. A specification tells you what the supplier intends to deliver, not what the bag in your hand contains. To judge a delivery, ask for that lot's certificate; to compare suppliers, use the specification.

Questions to ask of every number on the page

Every value in a report should survive the questions below. If it cannot answer them, ask the supplier before you draw a conclusion from the number.

What is the unit?

A figure without a unit is not a result. Salt reports mix percent and parts per million freely, and the two are far apart in size. For a solid reported by mass, 1 % equals 10,000 ppm, and 1 ppm equals 1 mg/kg, as the University of Florida IFAS Extension sets out. A result written as "0.1" means one thing in percent and a very different thing in mg/kg, so read the unit column first.

Is it as-fed or dry-matter?

This is the basis, and it changes the number. As-fed results include the sample's moisture; dry-matter results are recalculated after that moisture is excluded, so they are higher and comparable between samples with different moisture content. Mississippi State University Extension gives the arithmetic plainly: if a sample is 50 % dry matter, its dry-matter concentration is twice its as-fed concentration. Compare results on the same basis or the comparison is meaningless.

What was the method, and what was its detection limit?

Analytical methods differ in sensitivity, and that shows up in the smallest values on the page. "Not detected" or "below the reporting limit" does not mean zero; it means the method did not report a quantified result at or above that level. A report can quietly fail here: if the laboratory's reporting limit is higher than the legal maximum you are checking against, the test cannot demonstrate compliance either way. Ask what method was used and what its reporting limit was.

Which standard is it being compared against?

A number only means something next to a requirement, and the requirement depends on the standard, its edition, and the jurisdiction. Food-grade salt standards and feed-grade specifications are separate documents written for separate uses, so a limit quoted for a cooking salt does not automatically describe a livestock salt. Ask which standard the sheet is judged against.

Is the number a maximum or a typical value?

A maximum is a ceiling used for a compliance decision. A typical value is a representative or observed result from a sample set. A typical value is not proof that every lot clears a legal maximum, and a maximum is not what a typical lot contains.

When a supplier quotes a single number without labeling it, ask which one it is. If it is a typical value, ask how many lots it covers. If it is a maximum, remember that it describes a limit rather than a measurement.

Purity figures are not a measurement of your bag

Purity is usually expressed as sodium chloride content, quoted on a dry-matter basis, exclusive of additives. Codex's standard for food-grade salt sets a minimum of 97 % sodium chloride on that basis (CXS 150-1985). Treat that as a minimum standard for food-grade salt rather than a measured result, and confirm the current text of the standard before quoting it in your own documents.

That figure does three things worth noticing. It is a floor, not a description of a particular bag. It is stated on a dry-matter basis, so it is not directly comparable to an as-fed result. And because it excludes additives, it is not a statement about anything added to the salt after processing. Purity is also a food-grade concept; a livestock salt is specified for its intended use, which is a different question.

What a salt report cannot tell you

Metal limits are jurisdiction-specific

Metal limits are the part of a report most often quoted out of context: limits come from regulations that apply in one place, to one category of product, in one edition. A 2023 peer-reviewed review of the European Union's contaminants regulation reports maximum lead levels of 1 mg/kg for salt generally and 2 mg/kg for specified unrefined salts, and a mercury limit of 0.10 mg/kg; the review is published in Toxics and describes Regulation (EU) 2023/915.

Those are European limits for the products the regulation covers, not universal limits, and a limit from one standard or edition should not be printed as another's. The same care applies in the United States, where two citations are commonly mixed up: 21 CFR 100.155 is the salt labeling rule, covering statements such as iodized or free flowing, and 21 CFR Part 165 is the beverages part of the code. Neither is a numeric heavy-metal panel for salt.

An element count is not a mineral count

Analytical reports measure elements, and an elemental assay does not count mineral compounds. One peer-reviewed study of 31 retail pink salts measured 25 nutritive and non-nutritive elements, and another study of commercial table salts measured 12 elements in its single Himalayan pink sample. Those are counts of what each study looked for, not a fixed property of pink salt; they differ because the studies asked different questions with different methods.

A report listing twenty-odd elements is therefore not evidence of a mineral total of 84, however often that figure appears in salt marketing. If a document makes a claim about how many minerals a salt contains, ask which substances were measured, at what detection limits, and in how many samples.

A short checklist before you rely on a report

Ask for the lot's certificate of analysis, the specification the product is judged against, the basis of the results, the units, the method and its reporting limit, and the jurisdiction whose limits apply. Keep the documents with your purchase records so you can compare the next lot against the last, and compare two salts only on the same basis and against the same standard.
