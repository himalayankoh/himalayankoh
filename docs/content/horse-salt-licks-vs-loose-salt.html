Salt is the simplest way to put sodium and chloride in front of an animal, and it is also the part of the ration most often left to chance. Owners ask a practical version of the same question: should I hang a lick block, or should I put out loose salt and let the animal decide how much it takes?

The answer starts with what salt does in the body, because that decides how much matters. After that, it comes down to which form you can actually monitor. Both work; they make different things easy.

Why the diet has to supply salt every day

Sodium and chloride keep body fluid volume and acid–base balance in range and carry nerve signals and muscle contractions. Chloride is also used to make hydrochloric acid in the stomach. Animals hold very little of either in reserve, so a regular supply in the diet is not optional; when sodium runs short, feed intake can fall or become irregular and growth can slow down (Mississippi State University Extension).

That is the whole nutritional case for salt, and it is worth saying plainly: salt is a sodium and chloride source. It is not a mineral supplement, and it does not replace one. University of Georgia Extension puts the requirements for the trace minerals cattle most often run short of — copper, zinc and selenium — in parts per million, while sodium and chloride are supplied in grams per day. A salt block cannot close a gap of that size.

How much salt, and why the published numbers differ

Cattle requirements are usually written as dietary sodium rather than a spoonful per animal. Mississippi State University Extension gives 0.06–0.08 % of the diet for growing and finishing cattle and dry gestating cows, and 0.10 % for lactating cows, and notes that chlorine requirements are not well determined.

Intake estimates vary because they describe different situations, so read them as estimates rather than picking the smallest number as a rule:

  • Oklahoma State Extension puts a mature cow at less than 1 oz of salt per head per day.
  • University of Georgia Extension offers a rule of thumb of 0.005–0.010 % of body weight as salt per day, roughly 1.0–1.9 oz for a 1,200 lb cow.
  • Missouri Extension observed free-choice loose salt intake of ¾–1⅓ oz per head per day, and about half that intake from a block in the comparison it cites.

For horses, the Merck Veterinary Manual cites a National Research Council ration recommendation of 1.6–1.8 g of salt per kg of feed dry matter and notes that the data behind salt requirements are limited. Purdue Extension gives older general guidance of about 1 oz per day. Oklahoma State Extension says 2 tablespoons is generally sufficient for an exercising horse, with more likely needed in hot, humid weather. Salt need rises with sweat loss, so workload and climate belong in the calculation.

What each form makes easy

A block is tidy. It does not blow away, it is easy to see, and you can tell at a glance whether animals are using it. What a block does not give you is a clear number. Blocks slow intake compared with loose salt, and University of Georgia Extension recommends loose salt where adequate consumption matters — it names cattle at risk of grass tetany. Missouri Extension's comparison found about half the intake from a block that it found from loose salt.

Loose salt is the reverse: easier for animals to take in the quantity they want, easier to measure by what disappears from the feeder, and easier to spoil if the feeder collects rain.

Horses add a complication of their own. Salt intake varies widely between individuals — the Merck Veterinary Manual reports 19–134 g per day in one study — and loose salt typically produces greater intake than a block. University of Kentucky Extension warns that horses use mineral blocks inconsistently, so it is a mistake to assume the block in the paddock is covering an individual horse's needs.

One claim is worth avoiding: you will read that a hard lick can never supply enough sodium. That is stronger than the published guidance supports. What the sources do support is that intake from a block can be slower, lower and harder to judge, which matters most for animals with a high need.

Where a salt source belongs when you feed a mineral mix

This is the part that catches people out. Nebraska Extension warns that offering plain salt separately from a free-choice mineral mix can reduce and destabilize mineral intake, because some animals eat the salt and others eat the mineral. Salt is often already built into cattle mineral mixes, and it frequently drives how much of the mix animals consume.

University of Minnesota Extension gives the practical version for horses: if you use a trace-mineral salt, do not offer another salt source alongside it, and if you feed a complete mineral supplement or a fortified grain mix, offer a separate plain salt block. Follow the directions on your mineral product and your nutrition plan rather than adding salt on top of a mix that was already formulated to be the whole supply.

Keeping a block usable outdoors

Salt dissolves in water, so the weather you cannot control decides how long a block lasts. University of Georgia Extension says a mineral feeder should keep the product dry and should be checked at least once a week; Nebraska Extension makes the same point about protecting feeders from rain and wind. A block kept off the ground, out of standing water and sheltered from direct rain holds up better than one sitting in a puddle.

Be careful with durability claims. There is no published service life for a rock salt block, and no authoritative comparison of block against loose salt in weather. Take shelter as practical guidance for your own paddock, and replace or reposition a block when it is visibly wasting away.

Water does more work than the salt

The Merck Veterinary Manual is direct on this: salt toxicosis is unlikely when the animal's sodium regulation is intact and fresh drinking water is continuously available. Risk rises with a failed waterer, overcrowding, frozen or unpalatable water, or a sudden restriction in water, and horses that are rarely affected by classic toxicosis can still be affected when a rise in salt intake is followed by a cut in water. The manual reports an acute oral lethal dose of about 2.2 g of salt per kg of body weight for cattle and horses — a veterinary toxicology figure, not a feeding limit, and not a number to plan around.

Introduce salt gradually to animals that have been without it, and keep clean water available at all times. That single habit prevents more trouble than any choice between a block and a bag.

Choosing for your own setup

If you can check a feeder often and know roughly what your animals eat, loose salt gives you the clearest picture of intake. If you need something that stays put, a lick works, provided you watch how much of it is actually being used and keep water available. Neither choice removes the need for a supplement formulated for your species and production stage.
